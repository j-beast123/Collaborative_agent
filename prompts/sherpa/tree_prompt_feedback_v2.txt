You analyse a Rubric-Generator system prompt. For completed physician-Agent clinical discussions, the generator read the
discussion, its outcome (success or failure) and the physician's feedback, and wrote ONE rubric for that discussion.

Valid rubrics that apply to at least one Agent reply in their source discussion are tested by intervention: at each
matching reply ("interventions"), on a success the reply was replaced by the rubric's level-0 behavior, on a failure by
its level-2 behavior, and the discussion was rerun several times. "rerun_outcomes" are those rerun results (1 = correct
final decision); "per_node_R_O" is the share of reruns at that reply that flipped the outcome, and R_O is their mean
over the matching replies. Invalid rubrics (see "problems"), rubrics removed by a validity gate, and rubrics that match
no reply ("verification": "no_match") receive no rerun and no R_O, and contribute 0 to the prompt's score. A rubric is
effective when its intervention actually changes the outcome.

Diagnose the PROMPT, not individual cases:
1. Which wording or omissions lead to rubrics whose interventions do not change outcomes?
2. Which wording leads to rubrics that do change outcomes?
Then give the requested number of distinct, general revision directions.

Never quote or paraphrase case content: no medical facts, option texts, answer letters, diagnoses or drug names.
Return only JSON: {"diagnosis": "<concise diagnosis>", "directions": ["<direction>", ...]}

Rubrics are kept per case and per outcome: each trajectory either got a NEW rubric ("assignment": "CREATED_NEW") or was
attached to an existing rubric judged to represent the same behavioral reason ("REUSED_EXISTING"); "canonical_rubric_id",
"members" and "assignment_rationale" show this lineage, and each member's R_O is that rubric's intervention on that
trajectory. Also diagnose the prompt's reuse behavior where the evidence shows it: incorrect merges (different reasons
attached to one rubric), missed duplicates (the same reason given a new rubric), over-generalized rubrics and
applicability drift. A member whose rerun did not change the outcome is not by itself evidence of a bad merge, and fewer
rubrics are never better for their own sake.
